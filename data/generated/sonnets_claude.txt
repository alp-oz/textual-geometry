I

When morning lays her fingers on the sea
And drives the silver from the harbour wall,
I count the hours that still belong to me,
And find them fewer than I would recall.
The gulls that wheel above the idle boats
Have no concern for what the tide has done;
They cry as if the world were all their notes,
And sing the same to moon and to the sun.
So let me learn of them to take the day
Without a reckoning of its fading light,
To spend it as the wave that rolls away
And leaves no debt upon the sand at night:
For he who counts the grains will lose the shore,
And he who loves the shore will count no more.

II

My love is like a lamp in a cold hall,
That throws a little warmth and no great flame,
Yet makes the shadows on the plaster fall
In shapes that seem to call me by my name.
I would not trade it for a sun at noon,
Whose burning leaves no corner for the eye,
Nor for the pale and calculating moon
That lends her light and keeps it from the sky.
Small is the circle that my lamp can mend,
And poor the oil that feeds its steady glow,
But where it burns I find a faithful friend,
And where it fails I know what dark can show:
So let it burn, though winds should shake the door,
And keep one room unfrightened evermore.

III

Why should the poet praise the rose again,
Whose petals every spring have lost their sound,
When there are lines of grief and lines of pain
That no one yet has fastened to the ground?
I would sing rather of the broken plough
That rusts at evening in the furrowed field,
Of the old man who wipes his weathered brow
And thinks of what the stubborn seasons yield.
There is a beauty in the work half done,
In bread unbaked and in the unspun thread,
In all that waits beneath the setting sun
For hands that cannot rest till they are dead:
Let others have the rose and its renown;
I choose the furrow and the worn-out gown.

IV

Thou hast a way of silence that I fear
More than the sharpest word that ever fell,
For in the pause I hear what I hold dear
Go out like candles in a vacant cell.
If thou wouldst chide me, chide me to my face,
And let thy anger break upon my head;
But do not leave me in this empty space
Where all the answers I have sought are dead.
The silence of a lover is a sea
That has no shore and yields no sounding line,
And he who drowns in it will never be
Allowed the mercy of a single sign:
Speak, then, though cruel be the words thou choose;
A sentence spoken is a thing to lose.

V

The merchant counts his gold behind the door
And trembles at the footstep of the wind;
He loves each coin as miser loves the poor
Whose pennies he has taken and then pinned.
But I, who have no treasure in my chest
Save one thin ring and some unfinished rhyme,
Sleep sounder than the richest or the best,
Nor dread the thief who comes by night or time.
For what a man has not he cannot lose,
And what he lacks he cannot fear to miss;
The beggar's road is free to him who choose
To walk it, and the beggar's bread is bliss
Beside the feast of one who sits in dread
Of every knock that wakes him in his bed.

VI

Sweet sleep, that comest only when unsought
And fleest from the one who calls thee most,
Why dost thou play with every anxious thought
And leave me as a sentry at his post?
I have besieged thee with the cooling night,
With poppies, and with water, and with prayer,
With every charm that promises delight
And every drug the wise physicians bear.
Yet still thou standest distant as a star
That shines upon the sleepless and the sad,
And mocks the one who wanders near and far
Without the hope of anything he had:
Come, gentle sleep, and take my weary hand,
And lead me to thy soft and silent land.

VII

The winter came before the leaves were down
And caught the orchard in its yellow gown;
The apples hung like lanterns in the snow,
And no one came to bid them ripen slow.
So thought I of the friends who went away
Before the season of their work was done,
And of the songs that none will ever play
Because the singer perished with the sun.
Yet there is comfort in the frozen bough:
It keeps the shape of summer in its bones,
And when the spring returns, as it will now,
The sap shall rise among the silent stones:
So may the dead, though stiffened by the frost,
Be found again in what we thought was lost.

VIII

If I should die before the year is old,
Remember not my failings but my song,
Which, though it lacked the brightness of the gold,
Was honest, and was never heard for long.
Plant on my grave no marble and no yew,
But let the common grass grow where I lie,
And let some child, who never knew me, through
The summer afternoon go wandering by.
For fame is but the echo of a name
That louder voices swallow in their turn,
And only love, that asks for no acclaim,
Can keep the small flame of a life to burn:
So let me be remembered, if at all,
By one who loved me best and loved me small.

IX

The bells of evening call the fields to rest,
And every furrow sighs and settles low;
The labourer, with weariness oppressed,
Goes homeward through the dusk with footsteps slow.
Behind him falls the shadow of the wall,
Before him glows the window of his door,
And in that little light he finds his all,
His bread, his fire, and a warmer floor.
What king has ever had a richer hour
Than he who turns the latch and hears his name,
Who feels his children's laughter like a shower
Upon a land that long has known the flame?
Let towers fall and empires turn to dust:
The latch, the lamp, the loaf are what we trust.

X

I saw a ship go down in sight of land
With all her canvas set and colours flying,
And on the shore stood many, hand in hand,
Who watched the sea receive her and her dying.
No cry went up, for none could reach so far;
The wind was loud, the waves were strong and high,
And one by one each light and every spar
Went under, and the water closed the sky.
So go the proud designs that men have made,
The lofty hopes that hover on the foam,
The vows that in a summer evening stayed
And in the winter never found their home:
Yet from the wreck some planks are driven in,
And on them love may float and then begin.

XI

Thy beauty is a field where none have trod,
A morning where no footprint has been laid,
And I, who dare not touch it, praise it, odd
As that may seem, with silence unafraid.
For praise that comes in clamour spoils the grace
Of what it seeks to honour and to keep;
The painter's hand that hastens to the face
May wake the sleeper from her quiet sleep.
Let others carve thy likeness in the stone,
Or fix thee in a verse of gilded lines;
I would not make thy loveliness my own,
But watch it as the sun on window shines:
A light that asks not why it comes to be,
And shines the more for being let go free.

XII

The scholar in his study turns the page
And finds the same old question in the same
Dim ink that troubled every earlier age
And left the wisest wondering at the claim.
What is the truth, and who may hold it fast?
It slips like water through the sieve of thought;
We catch a glimpse of it, and then at last
We find we have recorded what we ought.
Yet still he reads, and still he lights his lamp,
And still the night is long, the fire low,
And in the corner of the room, the damp
Creeps up the wall where all old volumes go:
So seeks the mind its own unlikely end,
And finds a friend in every book, a friend.

XIII

O gentle jealousy, thou green-eyed guest
That sittest at my table, quiet and thin,
Why dost thou haunt a heart that is at rest
And whisper of the shadow come within?
I know her smile is only meant for me,
I know her letters speak a faithful hand,
Yet when she laughs with others, I can see
Thy fingers loosening the golden band.
Depart, thou counsellor of empty fears,
Who feedest upon what is never there,
And leave me to the comfort of my years
And to the trust that bids me not despair:
Yet if thou must remain, then keep thy place,
And do not look her boldly in the face.

XIV

When I was young I thought that time was wide
And lay before me like a summer plain;
I did not see the river at my side
That ran toward the sea, and ran in vain.
Now every year goes by more swift than thought,
And every friend is farther than before,
And all the things I fancied I had bought
Are shown as borrowed, and are owed once more.
Yet I would not return to that green morn,
Nor change the burden for a lighter load;
For every wound and every year has worn
A deeper channel in the stony road:
And he who walks it, weary though he be,
Has learned the meaning of the sea.

XV

The soldier home from war sits by the fire
And does not speak of what his eyes have seen;
His hands, once quick to answer his desire,
Now rest like stones upon the cloth of green.
His wife looks at him, and her gaze is kind,
And yet she dares not ask him where he went;
She knows some things are better left behind,
And some sad answers never can be spent.
The children play beside him on the floor
And tug the cuff that once was stained with red;
He smiles at them, and wishes nothing more
Than quiet bread, a roof, a gentle bed:
Let no one tell him that the fight was just;
He bears it in his bones, and in the dust.

XVI

Sing not to me of conquests and of fame,
For I have seen what both have left behind:
A trampled field, a town consumed by flame,
A mother searching for her child, half blind.
The trumpet and the banner and the drum
Are bright upon the morning of the march,
But when the ranks of silent evening come,
There is no laurel for the broken arch.
I sing of ploughmen and of patient wives,
Of hands that mend and hearths that keep the heat,
Of those who build the quiet of our lives
And ask no monument beneath their feet:
For they, and not the heroes of the sword,
Have made the world a place to be adored.

XVII

The clock upon the wall repeats its tale
In tones too low to hear, too sure to doubt;
It cares not for the hopeful or the frail,
But turns its wheels and carries all about.
I once believed that I might bribe the hours
With work, and wealth, and promises of love,
But they are deaf to gold and deaf to flowers,
And bow to nothing in the realms above.
So now I sit and listen to its beat,
And let the minutes pass me like a stream,
And find, in this surrender, something sweet:
A peace I could not reach by any dream:
For he who ceases wrestling with the clock
Is free to hear the music of its knock.

XVIII

Come, let us walk beside the river bank
Where willows lean to drink the evening air,
And speak of simple things, of what we drank
At supper, and of how the children fare.
Let no great theme disturb our gentle talk,
No grief nor glory trouble the soft hour;
We have no need to prove or plan or balk,
But only to be present, like a flower.
The water carries on its glassy back
The images of clouds we shall not see
Again, and every leaf that leaves its track
Is gone as softly as it came to be:
And in that passing we are well content
To lose the day and not to lose the bent.

XIX

Let not the rival poet's gilded phrase
Persuade thee that my verses are but plain;
For he who dresses every line in praise
Has little left to give when praise is vain.
My words are homespun, cut for common wear,
And wear the better for their honest thread;
His silks are fine to see, but cannot bear
The rain that falls on every living head.
I do not ask for laurels or applause,
Nor that my name be carried by the throng;
I ask but that thou keep the humble cause
Of one who loved thee well and loved thee long:
And if a better singer take my place,
Let him not love thee less, nor lack thy grace.

XX

Thy hair is not of gold, thy lips not red
As coral, nor thy cheeks as roses fair;
The things that poets praise are all unsaid
Of thee, who art as plain as common air.
And yet I would not have thee other made,
For every plainness is a gift to me,
And I who love thee need not be afraid
Of any rival's finer pedigree.
The woman painted by the poets' art
Is but a mirror of the poets' mind,
But thou art real, and hast a living heart
That beats for me and leaves the dream behind:
So let the world be dazzled by the sham;
I love thee for the thing that thou dost am.

XXI

The sea remembers every ship that sailed
And every sailor lost beneath its tide;
It keeps them in the deep where light has failed
And holds them in a silence wide and wide.
The shore forgets, and builds again its town,
And fishers cast their nets above the grave,
And children play where sinking men went down
And do not hear the whisper in the wave.
So let it be: the living must go on,
And dead men would not ask us to repine;
Yet when the moon is full and wind is gone
I think of them, and pour a little wine:
A drop for those who sleep beneath the foam,
And one for those who never found their home.

XXII

The beggar at the gate has seen the king
Ride by in splendour on a silver steed,
And wondered at the weight of every ring
And at the golden harness of his need.
For kings have needs that beggars never know:
They fear the knife, the cup, the whispered word,
And every friend is potentially a foe,
And every kindness suspect when it's heard.
The beggar sleeps beneath the open sky
And fears no assassin in the dark;
He dreams of bread, and when the stars go by
He wakes and hears the singing of the lark:
Which of the two, then, has the better part?
The one whose crown is weightless on his heart.

XXIII

I wrote thy name upon the frosted pane
And watched it vanish in the morning sun;
It left behind a trace of silver rain
And one thin line where all the letters run.
So fares the writing of a mortal hand
On every surface that the world supplies:
It fades like footprints on a stretch of sand
Before the tide that never sleeps or dies.
Yet I will write again, and write each day,
Though every line be washed away by noon;
For love is not the thing that stays, but they
Who make the effort underneath the moon:
And thou, who readest what I cannot keep,
Wilt know the writing by the tears I weep.

XXIV

The farmer's daughter, walking to the well,
Sang of a lover gone beyond the hill;
Her voice was like a gently ringing bell
That wakes the valley when the air is still.
She did not know that I, who passed that way,
Had also lost a love beyond the sea,
And in her song I heard what I would say
If speech were not a stranger unto me.
I thanked her with a silence and a bow,
And went my road, but carried in my ear
The sound of what I never could allow
Myself to utter in the hour of fear:
So songs are bridges, built by those who grieve,
On which another traveller may believe.

XXV

When all the flatterers have left the hall
And only candle-ends remain to burn,
I sit alone and listen to the call
Of conscience, who is not so quick to turn.
She tells me what the courtiers would not say,
That I have spent my gifts on idle show,
And let the better part of me decay
While chasing praise I did not wish to know.
I bow my head and do not make reply,
For every charge she lays is just and true;
And yet I find a comfort drawing nigh:
That one who judges is a friend to you:
So let her speak until the morning light,
And let me learn to do what she thinks right.

XXVI

If music be the food of love, then play
Until the strings are worn and I am fed;
But let the tune be tender, not the gay
And boisterous music of the tavern bed.
For love, like hunger, is a quiet thing
That grows by what it feeds on in the dark,
And every note the gentle lutes can bring
Is water to the root and to the bark.
Play on, then, till the candle gutters low,
And let the chords return and then depart;
I have no need to ask where music's go,
So long as they have left me with a heart:
And when the final string has ceased to sound,
Let silence be the sweetest note I've found.

XXVII

The mountain does not envy the low plain,
Nor does the river envy the high peak;
Each fills the place assigned, and in the rain
Each takes the share that neither has to seek.
Why, then, should I, a creature of the earth,
Complain of what my station has not lent,
Or sigh for ranks of greater worldly worth
When I am clothed and fed and well content?
The one who covets what he cannot own
Is poorer than the beggar in the street,
And he who loves the little he has known
Is rich in what he has, and rich in sweet:
Let me be glad of what the day has brought,
And spend no coin upon the bit I ought.

XXVIII

The doctor shakes his head and leaves the room,
And all the family stands pale and still;
There is no cure, no balm, no healing bloom
To stay the hand of an appointed ill.
The dying man looks up and smiles at them,
And bids them think of summers they have shared;
He hears no trumpet and no requiem,
But only that his dear ones have been spared.
So passes one more soul from sun and earth,
With little noise and not a single tear
Of his; the tears are left for those of worth
Who stay to mourn, and linger here:
And I, who watch, resolve to live as he,
And meet my end with quiet dignity.

XXIX

What use is wisdom if it brings no peace,
Or learning if it leaves the heart unfed?
The books I read increase my doubts, not cease
Them; and the more I know, the less I've said.
Yet I would not return to ignorance,
Nor wish the shutters closed against the sun,
For even sorrow has a kind of dance
That only those who know the steps may run.
So give me knowledge, though it cost me ease,
And give me doubt, if doubt be what I must;
I would not have the shelter of the trees
At the expense of walking in the dust:
For he who walks has seen a wider shore
Than he who stays and wishes nothing more.

XXX

The lighthouse keeper trims his lamp at dusk
And climbs the winding stair that knows his tread;
He does not hope for thanks, nor does he ask
For any wage but bread and one clean bed.
Each night he sends his beam across the deep
To warn the passing vessels of the rock,
And though he sees the sailors fall asleep
Or never see his light, he keeps the clock.
There is a love that asks for no return,
That serves the stranger whom it cannot meet,
And in the lamp that patiently will burn
I read the lesson of a task complete:
To serve unseen, to give and not to know
Whether the gift has helped the ones below.

XXXI

The market square is loud with trade and cry,
The butcher weighs his mutton, and the fish
Lie shining on their slabs, and those who buy
Dispute the price and argue as they wish.
I walk among them, hungry and alone,
And think how strange it is that all these folk
Have love and grief and fear that match my own,
And yet go on to barter and to joke.
Perhaps the noise is how we keep at bay
The silence that would otherwise be heard,
And every shouted bargain of the day
Is half a prayer disguised as haggled word:
So let them cry, and let me walk alone,
And think of what is common and our own.

XXXII

Fair youth, whose face I praise in secret lines,
Know that thy beauty is a borrowed thing,
That every grace of thine, though it outshines
The summer, will not wait for any spring.
But in my verse I give thee leave to stay,
And hold the dawn upon thy cheek for ever;
No wrinkle shall encroach, no hair turn grey,
Nor shall the grave thy gentle bloom dissever.
Yet this is but a trick of rhyming art,
A lie that love permits and truth forgives;
For I know well that time will play its part,
And that the poem lives where no man lives:
So read me when thou art as old as I,
And smile to see how love can cheat the sky.

XXXIII

The night is long and I am far from thee,
And every star that shines is cold and strange;
I lie awake and think of what might be
If distance were a thing that one could change.
I draw the curtain, watch the empty street,
And hear the watchman call the hour of three;
I hear the footfall of some wandering feet
That might be thine, but surely cannot be.
Come back, or send a letter by the morn,
Or let me dream thy face before I wake;
For I am weary, tired, and forlorn,
And sleep alone is poor for sorrow's sake:
Yet if thou canst not come, then let me say
That I will wait, though waiting last for aye.

XXXIV

The poor man's feast is bread and common wine
Shared with a neighbour at the table's end;
The rich man's feast is silver, gilt, and fine,
But nothing there is shared with any friend.
I know which supper I would rather eat,
And which would warm me better in the cold;
I'd take the crust of him who finds it sweet
Before the rich man's dish of rarest gold.
For hunger is the finest of all sauces,
And company the finest of all spice,
And he who sits at ease among the losses
Of lonely wealth has paid too great a price:
Give me a stool, a table, and a few
Who laugh at what I say and what I do.

XXXV

My poor heart is a house with many doors,
Of which I keep the keys and lose them all;
Through some there come the friends I would adore,
Through others, shadows lurking in the hall.
I try to lock the doors of grief and pain,
But still they open when the wind is high,
And I must sit and watch the gathering rain
Fall through the roof of an unfriendly sky.
Yet there is one small door I dare not close,
The door of hope, that stands a little ajar;
Through it there comes at times a fragrant rose,
And sometimes a long ray of a distant star:
So let the other doors be what they will;
I'll keep this open, and be hopeful still.

XXXVI

The proud cathedral lifts its spire to heaven
And casts a shadow on the sleeping town;
The masons who had built it, seven by seven,
Are dust, but still the stones keep their renown.
I wonder what they thought, those men of old,
Who laid each block and never saw it done;
They worked for ages yet unborn, and bold
Were they to build against the setting sun.
So let me labour in the lesser things,
And set my stone upon a wall unseen,
For he who builds for what the future brings
Is part of what has been and what has been:
And though my name be lost, my work may stand,
A little part of some great house of land.

XXXVII

O sweet disorder of the morning bed,
Where tangled linen holds a trace of thee!
I linger, though the sun is overhead,
And all the world awaits my company.
The work will wait, the letters will not run,
The bread will rise without my watching eye;
I only want to lie here in the sun
And let the leisurely, slow hours go by.
For idle moments are the gold of life,
The coins that we are given and may spend
Without regard to hurry and to strife,
And on no business that the world may send:
So spare me, duty; let me, for a while,
Lie in the warmth of thy remembered smile.

XXXVIII

The orphan child is standing at the gate,
Her bundle tied with string, her face too wan;
She does not know what future or what fate
Awaits her when the cold of night comes on.
The master of the house looks down and sighs,
And bids her enter, share the evening meal;
She lifts to him her wide and wondering eyes,
And does not dare to think that this is real.
O kindness, small and sudden, thou art more
Than all the sermons of the learned throng!
Thou openest to the beggar every door,
And makest weakness, for a day, grow strong:
So let me, when I am no longer poor,
Be one to open, and to keep the door.

XXXIX

The fisherman has mended all his nets
And leaves them drying on the harbour wall;
He sits and smokes and has no vain regrets,
Though fortune has not favoured him at all.
The sea has given what the sea may give,
And taken what it took without a word;
He asks no more than the right to live
By what the waters send him, undeterred.
There is a wisdom in his weathered face
That all the learned books can never teach:
The wisdom of the man who knows his place
Between the tide and the uncertain beach:
And I, who strive and fret and rarely rest,
May learn from him what he has known the best.

XL

When all the songs are sung and all the wine
Is drunk, and all the guests have gone to sleep,
I take the stage and watch the last lights shine
Upon the floor where dancers lately leap.
Then comes the cold, the silence, and the doubt:
Was it a joy, or only noise and show?
Did any heart, in all that merry rout,
Find something that it truly wished to know?
I cannot tell; the music is a dream
That fades before the morning comes to say
That nothing is exactly as it seem,
And every feast is but a passing day:
Yet I would dance again, and sing once more,
For joy is joy, though gone, and gone before.

XLI

The baker rises when the stars are bright
And kneads the dough before the village wakes;
His oven, glowing red against the night,
Is warmer than the bed that he forsakes.
By dawn the loaves are cooling on the shelf,
And children come with pennies in their hands;
He takes the coins and thinks about himself
As one of many, serving many lands.
For bread is more than flour, salt, and yeast;
It is the patience of a thousand years,
The labour of the hungry and the least,
The harvest that is salted by our tears:
And he who bakes it, early and alone,
Is priest of what the poorest call their own.

XLII

A letter came from one I thought was dead,
Its paper yellowed and its ink grown pale;
It said what in the past was left unsaid,
And bade me not to grieve nor yet to wail.
It spoke of gardens that I used to know,
Of apple trees and of a river bend,
Of how the summer light was bright, and so
It begged me to remember, as a friend.
I read it twice, and set it on my knee,
And looked into the fire's fading glow;
The years that stood between us seemed to be
A little thing, a wall that falls to show
The garden still is there beyond the gate,
Though all who walked in it have met their fate.

XLIII

The crow that sits upon the leafless bough
Looks down on all the folly of the field,
And sees the farmer bending to the plough
And hoping for the harvest it will yield.
He knows the weather better than the man,
And knows that frost will come before the sheaf;
He croaks a warning, but it is no plan
That men can heed, so deaf is their belief.
So goes the world: the watchers on the tree
Who see the coming storm and cry it out
Are scorned by those who plant and cannot see
What lies beyond the borders of their doubt:
And yet the crow endures, and so shall I,
Who sing of what the careless pass by.

XLIV

I envy not the eagle on the crag,
Nor the swift hawk that stoops upon its prey;
I would be as the swallow, who will lag
Behind the storm and find a gentler way.
She builds her nest beneath the cottage eaves
And asks no more than shelter and a fly;
She watches over her small brood, and weaves
A home of mud and straw against the sky.
There is a greatness in the humble bird
That has no need of conquest or of height,
Whose song is but a quiet, passing word
Of joy at the returning of the light:
So let me be as she, content to bring
My little share of music to the spring.

XLV

My mother's hands were rough with work and cold,
Yet gentle when they smoothed my feverish brow;
She never spoke of sorrow, nor of gold,
But hummed a tune I cannot sing it now.
I see her in the kitchen in the dawn,
Her sleeves rolled up, her hair tied with a string,
And hear the kettle singing, and the yawn
Of waking children, and the birds that sing.
O mother, long departed from this earth,
Thy love is still the measure of my days;
Thou gavest me a gift of greater worth
Than any worldly riches or praise:
And in my heart, wherever I may roam,
Thy kitchen is the best and only home.

XLVI

The shepherd on the hillside plays his reed,
And all the flock lies down to hear the tune;
He cares not if the town should pay no heed,
Or if his audience is but the moon.
He plays for love of music and the hour,
For solitude that has no need of praise,
And in the notes he finds a hidden power
To make a meadow of the longest days.
I envy him his unambitious art,
That asks no fee and seeks no distant goal,
And fills with quiet joy the lonely heart
Of one who is a shepherd of the soul:
So play, good shepherd, to the fading light,
And let thy music gentle me tonight.

XLVII

The wind that shakes the shutters of the house
Brings with it memories of other nights,
Of fire and laughter, of the gentle rouse
Of dogs and children at the early lights.
I lie and listen to its restless cry,
And think of all the voices that it bears:
The sailor's farewell and the mourner's sigh,
The prayers of those who dare not say their prayers.
Perhaps it carries mine as well, and flings
It out across the dark to find thine ear,
And whispers in thy sleep of simple things:
That I am here, and wish that thou wert here:
So blow, O wind, and take my words away,
And lay them gently where my loved ones lay.

XLVIII

The gardener prunes the roses in the spring
And cuts the living wood to make it grow;
It seems a cruelty, a savage thing,
To lop the branch that bore the summer's glow.
Yet he who knows the secret of the vine
Will tell thee that the pain is not in vain,
For only what is cut shall bloom and shine,
And only what is lost shall come again.
So may my griefs, that prune me to the core,
Be but the gardener's art within my breast,
And make me, in the season that's in store,
More fruitful than if I had been at rest:
For love, like roses, answers to the knife,
And pain, well borne, is but the root of life.

XLIX

I know not what the future has in store,
And have no wish to read the stars above;
I only know that I have loved before,
And that I shall again, for such is love.
The heart that has been broken is not dead,
But mends itself with patient, silent art,
And what was cracked is stronger in its bed
Than what was whole before it fell apart.
So fear not, trembling soul, the touch of pain,
Nor shun the open hand of fresh desire;
For every winter melts to spring again,
And every ash may kindle into fire:
Go forth, and love, and be as one reborn,
And take the roses, and accept the thorn.

L

The traveller who reaches the far shore
Looks back and sees the sea he has crossed,
And marvels at the waves he feared before,
And at the many nights he thought were lost.
They seem so small now, from the sheltered strand,
Those storms that nearly broke his mast in two;
He kneels and kisses the unmoving land,
And thanks the powers that brought him safely through.
So may it be with every soul that sails
The troubled waters of this mortal sea:
That when at last the final harbour hails,
We find the voyage was worth the misery:
And in that haven, resting from the foam,
We learn that every journey leads us home.

LI

When thou art gone, the house is full of thee:
Thy gloves upon the chair, thy cup, thy book,
The faint perfume of rosemary,
The fire that seems to hold thy gentle look.
I dare not move a single thing in place,
Lest I should break the spell that keeps thee near;
I walk the rooms and touch each little trace,
And find that every object is a tear.
Yet I will learn to live among these things
And make a calm of what was once a wound;
For grief, like love, upon its patient wings
Can carry us to where we may be found:
And I shall find thee, when my days are through,
In every room, in every thing I do.

LII

Now let the pen be laid upon the page,
And let the lamp be lowered to a spark;
I have no more to say to youth or age,
But only to the stillness of the dark.
My verses, such as they may be, are done,
And each of them has sung what it could sing;
Let them be read by one, or none, or none,
And let them rest, or let them take to wing.
For I have said the little that I knew,
Of love and time and sorrow and the sea,
And if there is a reader, may he view
In them a mirror, faint, but kind, of me:
So ends my book; so let the candle die,
And let the morning find me where I lie.
